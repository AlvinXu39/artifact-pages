September 2026 Supply Disruption India India Cotton Shortage Affects you

India’s cotton supply tightened. Yarn costs jumped 60%. Your EU margin window narrowed.

India’s domestic cotton crop fell roughly 10 million bales short of demand, driving yarn from Rs 250/kg to Rs 400/kg since early 2026 — a 60% surge that adds ~15% to Tiruppur knit production costs. Indian-origin knit garments now carry this cost headwind plus a 9.6% EU tariff; factories with direct cotton procurement face the raw material cost squeeze most immediately. Both pressures are live as of September 2026.

The shape of it, before the detail

Background

India’s 2025–26 cotton harvest came up roughly 10 million bales short of domestic demand. Spinning mills and knit exporters across India — led by Tiruppur — compete on the spot market for limited supply. Yarn moved from Rs 250/kg in January to Rs 400/kg by September 2026: a 60% rise that compresses per-unit margins for any factory buying on short-term contracts. The shortage is structural to this crop cycle and will not reset until the next crop is harvested.

What’s being affected

HS 61 knit garments — t-shirts, polo shirts, sweatshirts, leggings — carry cotton yarn at 50–70% of production cost and bear the direct impact. Factories procuring cotton directly feel the full 60% input price surge. Factories buying pre-spun yarn from Indian spinners absorb a lagged version of the same cost as spinners reprice forward contracts. Bangladesh and Pakistan competitors do not face this domestic shortage.

Likely impact

In the EU, India-origin knit garments already carry a 9.6% GSP tariff vs. Bangladesh’s 0% EBA rate. Combined with the 15% production cost headwind, India’s EU landed cost disadvantage widened by 20–25 points from the start of 2026 (verified from TARIFF-DB). This gap is large enough to prompt EU buyer programme reviews, not just price discussions — particularly for standard basics where Bangladesh qualification timelines are short.

See which buyers and product lines are most exposed, what this means for your factory’s competitive position, and where you have room to act.

Which buyers, which lines, which decisions

The event splits India’s knit exporters by cotton procurement model, by product line, and by export market — each dimension creates a different level of exposure and a different type of buyer response.

AWhich cotton procurement model carries the full cost hit
Direct cotton buyers, spot or short contractsVertical integrators and factories buying raw cotton directly from traders or on the spot market absorb the full Rs 250→400/kg move in real time. For verticals where cotton is 60–70% of COGS, the 60% input surge directly hits per-unit margin.
Yarn buyers on long-term supply contractsFactories that buy pre-spun yarn from spinners on multi-month fixed-price contracts absorb the surge at a lag — when contracts renew. If renewal is 3–6 months away, there is a cost management window that pure spot-market buyers do not have.
BWhich product lines carry the highest per-unit cost exposure
Cotton-intensive knit basicsT-shirts, polo shirts, sweatshirts — 50–70% cotton yarn content. These are the products buyers most frequently compare across origins. A 15% production cost increase here is both the largest absolute impact and the most visible to buyers running competitive tenders.
Technical or mixed-fibre stylesPerformance fabrics, polyester-cotton blends, or heavily processed garments carry lower pure cotton content as a share of cost. The input surge is partially offset by blending flexibility or the technical complexity that makes qualification switching difficult.
CHow the EU and US markets create different buyer pressure
EU-destined programmesEU buyers compare India (9.6% tariff + 15% cost increase) against Bangladesh (0% EBA, stable costs). The combined gap is 20–25 points — large enough for buyers to redirect H1 2027 allocations, especially for basics where Bangladesh factory qualification takes weeks, not seasons.
US-destined programmesIndia and Bangladesh pay the same US tariff rate (~26.5% under FL301). The US market comparison rests on production cost and qualification alone — a 15% cost increase is a real squeeze, but it does not add a tariff-widening penalty on top.
