Hormuz traffic down by a third. Suez shipping squeezed. Q4 apparel windows at risk.

Hormuz transit traffic fell to roughly 10 ships per day — a 33% drop from 15 or more weeks ago — as Iran simultaneously threatened a formal restricted-transit zone. Carriers on the Chittagong–Colombo–US East Coast corridor route through the Indian Ocean and Suez; a Cape reroute adds 12–14 days on a lane where Q4 seasonal programmes leave no slack. The disruption is live as of September 2026.

The shape of it, before the detail

Background

The Hormuz Strait channels tanker traffic from the Persian Gulf into the Indian Ocean. Reduced transits raise energy costs across the Indian Ocean–Suez corridor, and a formal restricted-zone declaration would force carriers to divert around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 12–14 days. Bangladesh RMG exports to US East Coast already sit at a tight Q4 delivery margin.

What's being affected

Asia–US East Coast via Suez is the most exposed lane for Bangladesh apparel exports — Chittagong to Savannah runs 25–28 days via Suez and 37–42 days via Cape. Woven and knit programmes on September–October vessel slots are already in the at-risk window. Europe-bound lanes via the same corridor face equivalent disruption.

Likely impact

Standard Suez routing gives Bangladesh factories 30-day door-to-floor lead times for US Q4 programmes. Cape rerouting pushes that to 42-plus days, forcing either earlier factory exit or a missed retail delivery window. Buyers with hard floor-set dates for holiday programmes will ask for confirmed ETAs in writing before they finalise allocations.
