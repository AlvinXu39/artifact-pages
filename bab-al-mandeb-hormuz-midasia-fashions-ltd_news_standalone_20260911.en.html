The shape of it, before the detail

Background

Houthi forces seized Mokha port on Yemen's Red Sea coast on September 10, collapsing Bab Al Mandeb traffic from ~30 ships/day to 6. Simultaneously, US forces and Iran's IRGC exchanged tanker strikes in the Arabian Gulf — 73 IMO-confirmed vessel attacks since February 2026, with the heaviest escalation in the week of September 8. Most ships are switching off transponders and rerouting to avoid attack. The Red Sea corridor — the primary route for Bangladesh-to-Europe garment exports — is effectively closed.

What's being affected

Bangladesh-to-EU apparel flows travel Chittagong → Red Sea → Suez Canal → Mediterranean → European ports. That entire corridor is disrupted. 514 commercial vessels were stuck in the Arabian Gulf on September 10; ships already past Hormuz are diverting around the Cape of Good Hope. Your EU zero-duty (EBA) tariff position is unchanged — this is a transit problem, not a tariff problem. But the transit extension is real and immediate.

Likely impact

The Cape of Good Hope reroute adds 14–21 days to Bangladesh-Europe transit time (HYPOTHESIS — based on 2024–2025 Red Sea disruption data). For a Bangladesh factory already running a 90–120 day cash cycle, that extension pushes receivables out to 105–141 days — large enough to create an acute working capital gap and to trigger EU buyer delivery penalty conversations.
