EU banned brands from destroying unsold clothes. Retailers must comply. Orders shrink.

The EU banned large companies from destroying unsold apparel, accessories, and footwear under the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR), effective 19 July 2026. Large companies — more than 250 employees or €50M revenue — must comply now; medium-sized companies follow in 2030. Brands that cleared excess stock by destroying it must now donate, resell, or recycle.

The shape of it, before the detail

Background

The EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR) — active since 2024 — introduced a ban on the destruction of unsold consumer textiles as part of its circular economy mandate. It applies to any company selling into the EU market, regardless of where it is headquartered or where its goods are made. Companies must disclose any unsold inventory destroyed under a permitted exception and retain documentation for five years.

What's being affected

All unsold apparel, clothing accessories, and footwear are in scope — the categories Bangladesh factories produce. Large EU companies (>250 employees or >€50M revenue) are banned from destruction from 19 July 2026. Medium-sized companies follow from 2030. Small and micro-enterprises are exempt. Permitted alternatives: donation, resale, repair, upcycling, certified recycling. Incineration and landfill of unsold textiles: out.

Likely impact

Until now, brands used overproduction and destruction as a quiet inventory buffer — order more than needed, destroy the excess, avoid markdowns. The ban removes that option for large companies immediately. Not enough to redirect where they source from — Bangladesh's cost position is unchanged. But enough for buyers to fundamentally change how they size orders: smaller initial commits, tighter forecasting, and faster reorder cycles become the default.

CWhat brands can do instead

Destruction is out. Permitted exits for unsold stock: donation to charity or social enterprise, resale through secondary markets, repair and upcycling, certified textile recycling. Each requires documentation. Brands must retain records for 5 years.

You make knit and woven garments in Bangladesh. Your EU buyers — H&M, Next — take roughly 42% of your revenue. Your US buyers (Gap, PVH, Kontoor) take the rest. The ban applies to your EU book — H&M and Next are large companies, in scope from day one.

The regulation doesn't touch you directly — it lands on your buyers. H&M and Next can no longer carry buffer stock they can't sell and destroy. Their response: smaller initial orders, tighter buy quantities, reorder triggers at sell-through milestones. The factories that respond fastest when the reorder call comes capture the volume that buffer orders used to lock in.

H&M's new problem is not your FOB price — it is holding stock it cannot move or destroy. Fast reorder turnaround is now a sourcing criterion, not just a KPI.
