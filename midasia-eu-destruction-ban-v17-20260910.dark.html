July 19, 2026 Trade Regulation European Union ESPR · Reg 2024/1781 Affects you

EU banned brands from destroying unsold clothes. Buyers must plan without a buffer. Reorder speed wins allocation.

The EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR) banned large companies from destroying unsold apparel, accessories, and footwear, effective 19 July 2026. Companies with more than 250 employees or €50 million revenue — H&M and Next both meet that threshold — must comply now; medium-sized companies follow in 2030. National authorities are checking compliance, fines apply, and brands must retain 5 years of destruction records.

The shape of it, before the detail

Background

The ESPR destruction ban is now in force and applies to companies selling into the EU, regardless of where goods are made. Brands must publish unsold-destruction data each year; public accountability is part of compliance.

What’s being affected

All unsold apparel, accessories, and footwear are in scope. Large companies are covered from 19 July 2026; medium-sized companies follow in 2030. Permitted alternatives include donation, resale, repair, upcycling, and certified recycling, with records retained for 5 years.

Likely impact

The ban removes the quiet inventory buffer large buyers relied on. H&M must show destruction figures publicly, making slow styles a brand liability. Reports from May and June 2026 flagged H&M Bangladesh order reductions; smaller initial buys and reorder-driven programmes are already emerging.

Who this hits, what changes for your factory, and what to do about it.

The ban covers all unsold apparel, accessories, and footwear held by large EU companies. The commercial split is by company size, product type, and permitted exit route.

AWhich companies must comply now
Now — 19 Jul 2026Large companies — more than 250 employees, or more than €50M revenue, or more than €25M in assets, meeting any 2 of 3 criteria. H&M, Next, Zara, M&S, and Primark qualify; national authorities can inspect and fine.
Later or exemptMedium-sized companies follow in 2030; small and micro-enterprises remain exempt. That exemption does not extend to large multinationals routing stock through smaller affiliates.
BWhich products are in scope
BannedAll apparel, clothing accessories, and footwear — knit garments, woven shirts, trousers, denim, outerwear, workwear, underwear, bags, belts, scarves, hats, shoes, and boots.
Not in scopeHome textiles, furniture, and non-apparel goods are not covered by this specific ban. Future ESPR delegated acts may extend coverage.
CWhat brands must do with excess stock
Destruction prohibitedPermitted exits are donation, resale, repair, upcycling, or certified textile recycling. Each route needs documented evidence.
Public record requiredBrands must retain destruction records for 5 years and publish annual unsold-destruction figures on their own website. Public visibility is part of enforcement.

You make knit and woven garments in Bangladesh. H&M and Next — your EU buyers — account for roughly 42% of your revenue. Gap, PVH, and Kontoor take the rest. Both EU buyers are large companies and in scope from 19 July 2026.

H&M started reshuffling its Bangladesh supplier base before the ban took full effect — industry reports from May and June 2026 flagged order reductions. Factories losing allocation share no confirmed reorder capability. Factories gaining share can put a specific lead-time number in a contract.

Your styles' sell-through now determines whether H&M keeps them in next season's programme — slow movers create public disclosure risk for the brand, not just inventory cost. Reorder-ready supply is a programme criterion, not a bonus.

Your US buyers are not in the day-one EU scope; the immediate commercial adjustment is concentrated in H&M and Next.

AWhat your buyers will do
H&M
Initial buys shrink; reorders open after sell-through clears
H&M must publish destruction figures annually. Slow styles become a public liability, so initial quantities shrink and reorder slots open only when sell-through data clears. Factories unable to respond in under 21 days lose volume.
Next
Shorter contracts require a documented reorder guarantee
Next is moving to rolling programmes with tighter windows. It needs a reorder turnaround number in the contract before signing the initial order; 21 days confirmed beats a vague 3–4 week range.
BWhat your competitors are doing
Push
Flexible Bangladesh factories lead with reorder capability before sign-off
Factories with reorder-capable setups are pitching H&M and Next before allocation closes, leading with lead-time guarantees rather than initial FOB price.
Shift
Turkey and Morocco price 2-week replenishment as inventory insurance
Near-shore factories accept higher FOB to offer shorter replenishment. H&M is weighing lower Bangladesh FOB against lower unsold-stock risk from near-shore supply.
CWhat you should do
High
Pitch sell-through evidence, not FOB price
H&M’s first question is whether the style moves. Documented past-style sell-through is now a stronger sales tool than leading with price.
High
Commit to a specific reorder lead time
Next and H&M are writing turnaround into contracts at season sign-off. If the confirmed number is 21 days, say 21 days; vague answers lose allocation.
High
Lead with approved styles, not new development
Approved styles move from initial order to reorder without sampling delay. Reorder-ready styles fit a buyer managing sell-through risk; new development does not.
Medium
Offer a tiered initial buy with a sell-through trigger
Example: 800-unit initial buy with a confirmed 1,500-unit reorder at 65% sell-through by week 8. This removes overstock risk before the order is placed.
