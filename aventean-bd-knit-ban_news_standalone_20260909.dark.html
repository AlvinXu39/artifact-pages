Sep 2026 Supply Policy Bangladesh Import Policy Order — Knit Fabric Affects you

Bangladesh bans knit fabric imports. Local mills become the only source. Sportswear and technical fabrics need certification.

Bangladesh's new Import Policy Order prohibits knit fabric imports to protect domestic mills. Standard cotton and commodity knit fabrics are now blocked at the border; specialized MMF, sportswear, and functional textiles can still be imported but only with an industry association recommendation and lien bank certification. BGMEA and BKMEA have publicly demanded the ban be withdrawn, but it is in force today.

The shape of it, before the detail

Background

The government issued an Import Policy Order blocking knit fabric imports after domestic mills lobbied that foreign fabric was undercutting their investments. The ban is immediate and applies at the border; importers without a prior letter of credit on record cannot clear existing consignments without the new exemption paperwork. The industry associations consider the ban commercially damaging and are pressing for reversal.

What's being affected

All standard knit fabric categories are now blocked for import into Bangladesh — cotton interlock, jersey, rib, and commodity blends. The only importable knit fabrics are specialized MMF, sportswear, and functional textiles, which require both an association recommendation letter and lien bank certification before shipment. Woven fabrics are not covered by this order.

Likely impact

Factories sourcing standard knit fabric from China, Korea, or Taiwan now depend entirely on domestic mills. Domestic mill capacity can cover commodity cotton knit but has limited scale and quality range for technical fabrics. The bottleneck is most acute for any factory running sportswear or performance products that cannot easily obtain the exemption certification before orders ship.

From the news to your factory — three floors

The ban draws three lines: what can and cannot be imported, which factories face disruption, and where the compliance burden lands.

Domestic-only sourcingFactories that fully switch to domestic mills avoid the certification burden — but must accept domestic pricing, availability, and lead time constraints in exchange.

You manufacture tech accessories and hardgoods in Bangladesh. If your product lines include any fabric-incorporated components — elastic bands, knit-based accessories, wearable goods, or any soft-goods packaging — your input sourcing is directly affected by this order.

If your production does not use knit fabric, the ban does not hit your inputs directly. But it strains the Bangladesh manufacturing ecosystem around you: factories that supply knit-incorporated components or accessories face input shortages, and any shared logistics or labour market effects ripple across the Dhaka industrial zone.

If any knit or elastic component touches your product, get the exemption certification started now before your next import shipment arrives at the border.

The ban may be reversed — BGMEA and BKMEA are actively lobbying — but sourcing decisions made now should assume the ban holds, not assume it lifts. Planning for a reversal that doesn't come costs more than planning for a ban that does.
