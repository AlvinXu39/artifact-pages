The shape of it, before the detail

Background

Houthi forces seized Mokha port on Yemen's Red Sea coast on September 10, collapsing Bab Al Mandeb traffic from ~30 ships/day to 6. Simultaneously, US forces and Iran's IRGC exchanged tanker strikes in the Arabian Gulf — 73 IMO-confirmed vessel attacks since February 2026, with the heaviest escalation in the week of September 8. The US "tanker-for-tanker" policy destroyed 5 Iranian crude carriers in one week; Iran reported damage to 2 US warships and 18 vessels. Ships are switching off transponders and rerouting to avoid detection.

What's being affected

Every cargo type that normally moves through the Red Sea or Hormuz — containers, crude oil, LNG, grain, coal, fertiliser — is affected. 514 commercial vessels were stuck in the Arabian Gulf as of September 10. The Bab Al Mandeb is the gateway for Asia-to-Europe and Asia-to-US East Coast container trade; Hormuz carries ~20% of global oil. Both choke-points near-shut simultaneously is unprecedented since the war began in February.

Likely impact

The Cape of Good Hope reroute adds 14–21 days to Asia-to-Europe transit time (HYPOTHESIS — based on 2024–2025 Red Sea disruption data). Oil at $103.80/barrel means higher bunker costs are already embedded in freight quotes. Extended transit means later invoice creation, later payment, and a longer gap between production cost and cash receipt — large enough to reopen financing conversations and potentially move sourcing decisions for future seasons if the blockage continues.
