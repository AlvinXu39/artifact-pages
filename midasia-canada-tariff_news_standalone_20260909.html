September 8, 2026 Trade policy Canada Canada Retaliatory Surtax Affects you

Canada’s new tariff hit US apparel hard. Buyers need Asian sources to fill the gap.

Canada imposed a 50% retaliatory surtax on US-origin apparel and textiles, effective September 8, 2026. US-origin goods now pay a 50-point penalty to enter Canada; Bangladesh, Vietnam, and China goods enter at unchanged MFN rates. The 32-point cost gap is live.

The shape of it, before the detail

Background

Canada’s 50% surtax is part of C$27.6 billion in dollar-for-dollar countermeasures against US Section 338 tariffs. Apparel, fabrics, and yarn made in the US and shipped to Canada now pay 50% at the Canadian border on top of standard duty. The US→Canada textile channel, worth roughly $5.1 billion annually, is structurally disrupted. No stated expiry date.

What’s being affected

In scope: all US-origin apparel (HS 61, 62), home textiles (HS 63), and furniture entering Canada. Not in scope: goods from Bangladesh, Vietnam, Cambodia, China, or any non-US origin — they enter at unchanged MFN rates (typically 18% for apparel). The dividing line is country of origin, not product type.

Likely impact

Before: US apparel entered Canada duty-free under USMCA. After: 50% surtax — a 50-point jump overnight. Asian-origin apparel stays at ~18% MFN. That opens a ~32-point landed cost gap between US-made and Asian-made goods in Canada. A gap this large does not just reopen conversations — it redirects orders. Canadian buyers who sourced from US manufacturers are actively looking for alternatives right now.

Who gets hurt, who gains — and what this means for your factory.

What this means, floor by floor

Canada’s 50% surtax applies to origin, not product type. Any apparel or home textile made in the US now carries a 50-point penalty landing in Canada; goods from every other major sourcing country are unaffected.

CWho bears the cost

US manufacturers are priced out of their largest export market. A 50-point tariff cannot be absorbed through margin compression — typical US apparel factory margins are 8–15%. Canadian buyers must either pay the higher cost or switch sourcing. Most will switch. Asian suppliers face no new cost — they gained a competitive window that did not exist before September 8.

You manufacture denim apparel, woven shirts, and outerwear in Dhaka, Bangladesh — 600,000 pieces per month from 2,100 workers. Your book is primarily EU buyers, with Canada a limited channel today.

Your goods enter Canada at the standard MFN rate — roughly 18%, unchanged. Canada’s 50% surtax only applies to US-origin goods. US-made denim shirts and woven pants — the same product categories you produce — just became 32 points more expensive to land in Canada than your equivalent goods. You are now 32 points cheaper than a US manufacturer on any Canadian buyer’s cost sheet, on goods you already produce at scale.

The Canadian buyers shopping for a new woven-apparel source are looking for factories exactly like yours — and they need one before the next buying season locks.

Other Bangladesh and Vietnam factories have the same 32-point advantage. The one that calls first and can prove capacity gets the RFQ. First-mover advantage in a market shift closes fast — competing factories are already making calls.
